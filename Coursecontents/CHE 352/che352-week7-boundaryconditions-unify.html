Topic 1
What Are Boundary Conditions?
1.1

Definition & Purpose

When you solve a partial differential equation (PDE) — such as the heat equation, diffusion equation, or wave equation — the general solution contains arbitrary functions or constants. Boundary conditions (BCs) are additional constraints that specify what happens at the edges (boundaries) of the domain. They turn the general solution into a unique, physically meaningful one.

Think of it this way: solving a PDE without boundary conditions is like solving a second-order ODE without initial conditions — you get a family of solutions. BCs pick the one that actually describes your physical situation.

In engineering, variables like temperature \(T\), concentration \(c\), and displacement are governed by PDEs. The domain has physical boundaries (walls, surfaces, edges) and the BCs tell us: what is the value of the variable there? or what is the flux there?

Physical context

For a variable \(c(x, t)\) (e.g. concentration, temperature) in a region from \(x = x_0\) to \(x = x_L\) — the boundary conditions specify behaviour at \(x = x_0\) and \(x = x_L\). The initial condition specifies the state at \(t = t_0\).

The three types of boundary conditions, each capturing a different physical scenario:

  • Dirichlet: the value of \(c\) is prescribed at the boundary
  • Neumann: the derivative of \(c\) (the flux) is prescribed at the boundary
  • Cauchy / Mixed: a linear combination of value and flux is prescribed

Topic 2
The Three Types of Boundary Conditions
2.1

Dirichlet Boundary Condition (Type 1)

The Dirichlet condition specifies the value of the primary variable at the boundary. If a wall is held at a fixed temperature, or a container has a fixed concentration at its surface — that is a Dirichlet condition.

Dirichlet Condition (Type 1)
\[ c(x_0, t) = c_0 \quad \text{and/or} \quad c(x_L, t) = c_L \]
The variable \(c\) is set to a known constant value at the boundary. Also called a "first-kind" boundary condition.
  c₀ ←──────────────────────────→ cL
  |                                |
  x₀ ←──────── domain ──────────→ xL

  c(x₀, t) = c₀        c(xL, t) = cL
   (fixed left)          (fixed right)

Real-world examples:

  • A metal rod held at 100°C at one end and 20°C at the other
  • A tank with fixed salt concentration at its inlet
  • A chemical reactor with prescribed reactant concentration at the feed boundary

2.2

Neumann Boundary Condition (Type 2)

The Neumann condition specifies the derivative of the variable at the boundary — it prescribes the flux. An insulated wall has zero heat flux, so \(\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} = 0\) there.

Neumann Condition (Type 2)
\[ \left.\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\right|_{x=x_0} = c_0 \quad \text{and/or} \quad \left.\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\right|_{x=x_L} = c_L \]
The normal derivative of \(c\) is prescribed at the boundary. Also called a "second-kind" boundary condition. Here \(c_0\) and \(c_L\) represent prescribed derivative values (not concentration values).

Real-world examples:

  • An insulated end of a rod: \(\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} = 0\) (no heat escapes)
  • A prescribed flux at a membrane surface
  • A symmetry condition at the centreline of a symmetric domain
⚡ Quick Check — Neumann Condition 2 questions

A Dirichlet: \(T = 0\)

What quantity does the Neumann condition prescribe at the boundary?
The derivative of the variable — i.e. the flux. Specifically: \(\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\big|_{\text{boundary}}\). This is in contrast to Dirichlet (which prescribes the value of \(c\) itself).
2.3

Cauchy / Mixed Boundary Condition (Type 3)

The Cauchy (or Mixed) condition combines both: it prescribes a linear combination of the value of \(c\) and its derivative. This arises naturally in convective heat transfer — where the surface condition relates the temperature gradient to the temperature difference.

Cauchy / Mixed Condition (Type 3)
\[ a\,c + b_k \left.\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\right|_{x=x_0} = v_0 \quad \text{and} \quad a\,c + b_k \left.\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\right|_{x=x_L} = v_L \]
A linear combination of \(c\) and \(\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\) is prescribed at the boundary. Also called a "third-kind" or Robin boundary condition.

Real-world examples:

  • Newton's law of cooling: \(-k\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial x} = h(T - T_\infty)\) — flux proportional to temperature difference
  • Robin boundary conditions in diffusion with reaction
  • Mixed conditions in structural mechanics: part fixed (Dirichlet), part with prescribed force (Neumann)
Type 1
Dirichlet
\(c = \text{prescribed}\)
Fixed value at boundary. Temperature, concentration.
Type 2
Neumann
\(\dfrac{\partial c}{\partial x} = \text{prescribed}\)
Flux prescribed at boundary. Insulated walls, symmetry.

Cauchy / Mixed

Combination of value and flux. Convective transfer, Robin conditions.

Topic 3
Introduction to the Finite Difference Method (FDM)
3.1

How FDM Works (Conceptual)

The Finite Difference Method (FDM) is a numerical technique for solving PDEs. Instead of finding an exact analytical solution, FDM approximates the derivatives in the PDE using differences between values at discrete grid points. The continuous domain is replaced by a mesh of points, and the PDE becomes a system of algebraic equations — one per grid point — that can be solved computationally.

  1. Discretise the domain. Divide \([x_0, x_L]\) into \(N\) equally spaced points with spacing \(\Delta x = \frac{x_L - x_0}{N}\).
  2. Replace derivatives with finite differences. The first derivative \(\frac{dc}{dx}\) at \(x_i\) becomes \(\frac{c_{i+1} - c_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}\). The second derivative \(\frac{d^2c}{dx^2}\) becomes \(\frac{c_{i+1} - 2c_i + c_{i-1}}{(\Delta x)^2}\).
  3. Apply boundary conditions. Substitute the Dirichlet, Neumann, or Mixed conditions at the boundary nodes.
  4. Solve the algebraic system. The result is a set of linear equations (a matrix system) solvable by standard methods.
Key Finite Difference Approximations
\[ \frac{dc}{dx}\bigg|_i \approx \frac{c_{i+1} - c_{i-1}}{2\Delta x} \quad \text{(central difference, 1st order)} \] \[ \frac{d^2c}{dx^2}\bigg|_i \approx \frac{c_{i+1} - 2c_i + c_{i-1}}{(\Delta x)^2} \quad \text{(central difference, 2nd order)} \]
These replace exact derivatives in the PDE at each interior grid point, converting the PDE into a system of algebraic equations.
3.2

How Boundary Conditions Enter FDM

Each BC type translates directly into the FDM framework:

  • Dirichlet: Set \(c_0 = c_0^{\text{prescribed}}\) and \(c_N = c_N^{\text{prescribed}}\) directly — no equation needed at boundary nodes.
  • Neumann: Use \(\frac{c_1 - c_{-1}}{2\Delta x} = \left.\frac{dc}{dx}\right|_0\) — introduce a ghost point \(c_{-1}\) and eliminate it using the BC.
  • Mixed: Substitute the combined condition \(ac + b\frac{\partial c}{\partial x} = v\) into the difference equation at the boundary node.
💡 Why this matters for CHE352

FDM is the bridge between the analytical methods you have studied all semester — power series, Legendre, Bessel, numerical integration — and real engineering computation. In practice, most PDEs governing heat, mass, and momentum transfer don't have clean analytical solutions. FDM (and related methods: FEM, FVM) are how engineers actually solve them.

Definition
State the three types of boundary conditions and what each prescribes.
Dirichlet (Type 1): prescribes the value of \(c\) at the boundary. \(c(x_0, t) = c_0\).

Neumann (Type 2): prescribes the derivative (flux). \(\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\big|_{x_0} = c_0\).

Cauchy/Mixed (Type 3): prescribes a linear combination of value and derivative. \(ac + b\frac{\partial c}{\partial x} = v\).
Application
An insulated end of a metal rod has no heat flux. Which boundary condition type applies, and what is its mathematical form?
Neumann (Type 2). No heat flux means no gradient: \(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial x}\bigg|_{\text{wall}} = 0\). The derivative of temperature is zero at the insulated end.
Mechanism
How does FDM convert a PDE into something solvable on a computer?
FDM replaces the continuous derivatives in the PDE with algebraic approximations (finite differences) evaluated at discrete grid points. This turns the PDE into a system of linear algebraic equations — one per interior grid point — which can be assembled into a matrix and solved numerically.
