Topic 1
The Maclaurin Series
1.1

The Maclaurin Series Formula

The Maclaurin series is a special case of the Taylor series where the expansion is centred at \(x = 0\). For a function \(y = f(x)\), if \(f\) is infinitely differentiable at \(x = 0\), we can write:

Maclaurin Series for y = f(x)
\[ y = y_0 + x\,y_1(0) + \frac{x^2}{2!}\,y_2(0) + \frac{x^3}{3!}\,y_3(0) + \frac{x^4}{4!}\,y_4(0) + \cdots \]
where \(y_r(0)\) denotes the value of \(\dfrac{d^r y}{dx^r}\) evaluated at \(x = 0\).

The idea is simple: once you know all the derivatives of \(y\) at \(x = 0\), you can reconstruct the full function as an infinite polynomial (the power series). This is powerful because for many ODEs, computing those derivatives is exactly what we can do — step by step via the recurrence relation from Week 1.

The Leibnitz-Maclaurin Method — How It Works

The Leibnitz-Maclaurin method turns any second-order ODE (at an ordinary point) into a computable power series solution. The five steps are:

  1. Start with the second-order ODE \(f(x)y'' + g(x)y' + h(x)y = 0\)
  2. Differentiate the equation \(n\) times using Leibnitz theorem (Week 1)
  3. Set \(x = 0\) to get the recurrence relation in terms of \(y_n(0)\)
  4. Use initial conditions \(y_0 = y(0)\) and \(y_1 = y'(0)\) to find all \(y_n(0)\)
  5. Substitute into the Maclaurin series — this is your power series solution
💡 Why power series? Many ODEs don't have solutions in terms of standard functions like \(\sin x\) or \(e^x\). But they can always be solved as a power series — an infinite sum of \(x^n\) terms — as long as the equation has an ordinary point at \(x = 0\) (i.e. the coefficients don't blow up there). Bessel's equation and Legendre's equation (Weeks 3 and 4) are famous examples.

What does it mean for \(x = 0\) to be an "ordinary point" of an ODE, and why does it matter for this method?
An ordinary point is a value of \(x\) where the leading coefficient of the ODE does not vanish (is not zero). It matters because the Leibnitz-Maclaurin method requires setting \(x = 0\) to obtain a useful recurrence — if the leading coefficient is zero there, the recurrence becomes degenerate (you get \(0 = 0\)) and the method fails. At a singular point you would need the Frobenius method instead.
Topic 1 Active Recall
Definition
Write the Maclaurin series formula for \(y = f(x)\) up to the 4th-degree term. What do the denominators represent?
\[ y = y_0 + x\,y_1(0) + \frac{x^2}{2!}\,y_2(0) + \frac{x^3}{3!}\,y_3(0) + \frac{x^4}{4!}\,y_4(0) + \cdots \] The denominators are factorials (\(n!\)). The numerator coefficients are the \(n\)th derivatives of \(y\) evaluated at \(x = 0\).
Mechanism
State the 5 steps of the Leibnitz-Maclaurin method in order.
1. Write the ODE.
2. Differentiate the ODE \(n\) times using Leibnitz theorem.
3. Set \(x = 0\) to get the recurrence relation for \(y_n(0)\).
4. Use initial conditions \(y_0\) and \(y_1\) to compute all \(y_n(0)\).
5. Substitute into the Maclaurin series to obtain the power series solution.
Application
Why does the power series solution of a 2nd order ODE split into two separate series — one involving \(y_0\) and one involving \(y_1\)?
Because second-order ODEs have two linearly independent solutions. The recurrence links \(y_{n+2}\) to \(y_n\), so even-indexed terms (\(y_0, y_2, y_4, \ldots\)) build from the initial value \(y_0\), and odd-indexed terms (\(y_1, y_3, y_5, \ldots\)) build from the initial slope \(y_1\). These form two independent series that combine into the general solution — satisfying both arbitrary constants of the 2nd order ODE.
Computation
For the recurrence \(y_{n+2}(0) = -(n+1)\,y_n(0)\) with \(y_0 = 1\), what is \(y_6(0)\)?
Step through the recurrence:
\(n=0\): \(y_2(0) = -(1)(1) = -1\)
\(n=2\): \(y_4(0) = -(3)(-1) = 3\)
\(n=4\): \(y_6(0) = -(5)(3) = -15\)

So \(y_6(0) = \mathbf{-15}\).

Worked Examples — Maclaurin Series Solutions
2.1

Full Example — \(a^2y'' + xy' + y = 0\)

Full Example
Solve \(a^2 y'' + xy' + y = 0\) using the Leibnitz-Maclaurin method, given \(y(0) = y_0\) and \(y'(0) = y_1\).
1
Differentiate the ODE \(n\) times using Leibnitz.
The equation is \(a^2 y'' + xy' + y = 0\).
— Term \(a^2 y''\): differentiates simply to \(a^2 y_{n+2}\)
— Term \(xy'\): Leibnitz gives \(xy_{n+1} + ny_n\)
— Term \(y\): differentiates to \(y_n\)
So: \[ a^2 y_{n+2} + x y_{n+1} + n y_n + y_n = 0 \] \[ a^2 y_{n+2} + x y_{n+1} + (n+1) y_n = 0 \]
2
Set \(x = 0\) to get the recurrence. \[ a^2 y_{n+2}(0) + (n+1)y_n(0) = 0 \] \[ \boxed{y_{n+2}(0) = -\frac{(n+1)}{a^2} y_n(0)} \]
3
Compute even-indexed derivatives (from \(y_0\)):
\(n=0\): \(y_2(0) = -\dfrac{1}{a^2} y_0\)
\(n=2\): \(y_4(0) = -\dfrac{3}{a^2} y_2(0) = -\dfrac{3}{a^2} \cdot \left(-\dfrac{1}{a^2} y_0\right) = \dfrac{3}{a^4} y_0\)
\(n=4\): \(y_6(0) = -\dfrac{5}{a^2} y_4(0) = -\dfrac{15}{a^6} y_0\)
4
Compute odd-indexed derivatives (from \(y_1\)):
\(n=1\): \(y_3(0) = -\dfrac{2}{a^2} y_1\)
\(n=3\): \(y_5(0) = -\dfrac{4}{a^2} y_3(0) = \dfrac{8}{a^4} y_1\)
\(n=5\): \(y_7(0) = -\dfrac{6}{a^2} y_5(0) = -\dfrac{48}{a^6} y_1\)
5
Substitute into the Maclaurin series: \[ y = y_0 + y_1 x + \frac{y_2(0)}{2!}x^2 + \frac{y_3(0)}{3!}x^3 + \frac{y_4(0)}{4!}x^4 + \cdots \]
\[ y = y_0\left[1 - \frac{x^2}{2!\,a^2} + \frac{3x^4}{4!\,a^4} - \frac{15x^6}{6!\,a^6} + \cdots\right] + y_1\left[x - \frac{2x^3}{3!\,a^2} + \frac{8x^5}{5!\,a^4} - \cdots\right] \] This is the general power series solution — two linearly independent parts, one multiplied by \(y_0\), one by \(y_1\).

Example 2 — \(x^2y'' + xy' + y = 0\) (Singular Point)

Example 2 — From notes (14/07/2025)
Solve \(x^2 y'' + xy' + y = 0\) using the power series method. Find \(y_n\) given initial conditions.
1
Differentiating \(n\) times: for \(x^2 y''\), Leibnitz gives \(x^2 y_{n+2} + 2nx\,y_{n+1} + n(n-1)y_n\).
For \(xy'\): \(xy_{n+1} + ny_n\). For \(y\): \(y_n\).
Combined: \[ x^2 y_{n+2} + (2n+1)x\,y_{n+1} + (n^2+1)y_n = 0 \]
2
At \(x = 0\): the equation becomes \(0 = 0\), which is a singular point. The Leibnitz-Maclaurin method breaks down here — you must use the Frobenius method instead (not covered in this week). This is why the equation type matters before choosing a method.
⚠️ Ordinary vs Singular Points The Leibnitz-Maclaurin method only works at an ordinary point — where the ODE's leading coefficient doesn't vanish. If \(x = 0\) is a singular point (leading coefficient = 0 there), use Frobenius. For CHE352, Dr. Salami focuses on ordinary points where the method applies cleanly.

Example 3 — Full Numerical Solution

Example 3 — Full numerical solution
For \(y'' + xy' + y = 0\) with \(y(0) = 1\), \(y'(0) = 0\), find the power series solution up to \(x^6\).
1
Differentiate \(n\) times: \(y_{n+2} + xy_{n+1} + (n+1)y_n = 0\)
At \(x = 0\): \[ y_{n+2}(0) = -(n+1)y_n(0) \]
2
With \(y_0 = 1\), \(y_1 = 0\):
\(n=0\): \(y_2(0) = -(1)(1) = -1\)
\(n=2\): \(y_4(0) = -(3)(-1) = 3\)
\(n=4\): \(y_6(0) = -(5)(3) = -15\)
All odd terms: zero (since \(y_1 = 0\))
\[ y = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{3x^4}{4!} - \frac{15x^6}{6!} + \cdots \] \[ y = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^4}{8} - \frac{x^6}{48} + \cdots \]
Topic 2 Active Recall
Computation
For \(y'' + xy' + y = 0\) with \(y_0 = 1\), \(y_1 = 0\), write out the first three non-zero terms of the series solution explicitly (with numerical coefficients, not factorial notation).
Using \(y_2 = -1\), \(y_4 = 3\), \(y_6 = -15\):

\[ y = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^4}{8} - \frac{x^6}{48} + \cdots \]
Check: \(\dfrac{3}{4!} = \dfrac{3}{24} = \dfrac{1}{8}\) ✓ and \(\dfrac{-15}{6!} = \dfrac{-15}{720} = \dfrac{-1}{48}\) ✓
Mechanism
Why does the equation \(x^2 y'' + xy' + y = 0\) cause the Leibnitz-Maclaurin method to fail at \(x = 0\)?
At \(x = 0\), the leading coefficient \(x^2\) vanishes, making \(x = 0\) a singular point. When you differentiate \(n\) times and set \(x = 0\), every term containing \(x\) disappears and the equation collapses to \(0 = 0\) — a trivial identity that gives no recurrence relation. You need the Frobenius method for singular points.
Application
In the general solution of \(a^2 y'' + xy' + y = 0\), why are there two separate series — one in even powers of \(x\) and one in odd powers?
The recurrence \(y_{n+2}(0) = -\dfrac{n+1}{a^2}\,y_n(0)\) links every term to the term two indices earlier. This means:

— Even-indexed derivatives \(y_0, y_2, y_4, \ldots\) form a chain starting from \(y_0\).
— Odd-indexed derivatives \(y_1, y_3, y_5, \ldots\) form a separate chain starting from \(y_1\).

The two chains never interact, so the solution naturally splits into an even-power series (multiplied by \(y_0\)) and an odd-power series (multiplied by \(y_1\)) — corresponding to the two linearly independent solutions of the 2nd order ODE.
