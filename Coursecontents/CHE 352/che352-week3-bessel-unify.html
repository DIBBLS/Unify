Topic 1
Bessel Equation — Standard Form & Bessel Functions
1.1

Standard Form & Bessel Functions

A second order differential equation that occurs frequently in branches of technology is the Bessel equation, which is of the form:

Bessel Equation — Standard Form
x²y″ + xy′ + (x² − v²)y = 0
where v is a real constant

Expressing the solution in terms of gamma functions, the Bessel function of the first kind of order v, denoted Jᵥ(x), is:

Bessel Function Jᵥ(x)
Jᵥ(x) = (x/2)ᵛ · [ 1/Γ(v+1) − x²/[2¹·(1!)·Γ(v+2)] + x⁴/[2⁴·(2!)·Γ(v+3)] − ··· ]
Valid for v not a negative integer.

1.2

Bessel Functions

The complementary Bessel function of order −v is defined by replacing v with −v in the series expansion:

Bessel Function J₋ᵥ(x)
J₋ᵥ(x) = (x/2)⁻ᵛ · [ 1/Γ(1−v) − x²/[2·(1!)·Γ(2−v)] + x⁴/[2·(2!)·Γ(3−v)] − ··· ]
Valid provided v is not a positive integer.

When Jᵥ(x) and J₋ᵥ(x) are linearly independent (i.e., when v is not an integer), the general solution of the Bessel equation is:

y = A·Jᵥ(x) + B·J₋ᵥ(x)

⚡ Quick Check — Bessel Functions 1 question
What is the key structural difference between Jᵥ(x) and J₋ᵥ(x)?
Jᵥ(x) carries a leading factor of (x/2)ᵛ and uses Γ(v+1), Γ(v+2), … in its denominators; it is valid when v is not a negative integer. J₋ᵥ(x) carries (x/2)⁻ᵛ and uses Γ(1−v), Γ(2−v), …; it is valid when v is not a positive integer. Their leading powers and gamma-function arguments are mirror images of each other, and their validity conditions are opposite.
Topic 1 Active Recall
Definition
What is the standard form of the Bessel differential equation, and what does the symbol v represent?
The standard form is x²y″ + xy′ + (x² − v²)y = 0, where v is a real constant. The equation is second order and arises frequently in applied mathematics and engineering — particularly in problems with cylindrical symmetry.
Mechanism
According to the notes, what are the specific conditions required for the Bessel functions Jᵥ(x) and J₋ᵥ(x) to be valid?
Jᵥ(x) is valid provided v is not a negative integer. Conversely, J₋ᵥ(x) is valid provided v is not a positive integer. These restrictions arise from the gamma function: Γ(n) is undefined when n is zero or a negative integer, so certain terms in the series would blow up if the condition were violated.
Comparison
What is the key structural difference between Jᵥ(x) and J₋ᵥ(x) in their series expansions?
Jᵥ(x) carries a leading factor of (x/2)ᵛ and uses Γ(v+1), Γ(v+2), … in its denominators. J₋ᵥ(x) carries (x/2)⁻ᵛ and uses Γ(1−v), Γ(2−v), … Their validity conditions are also opposite: Jᵥ requires v not a negative integer; J₋ᵥ requires v not a positive integer.

Topic 2
When v = n (Integer) — Jₙ(x), J₀(x) and J₁(x)
2.1

Integer Order — Jₙ, J₀, J₁

When v = n, where n is a non-negative integer, the gamma functions in the series reduce to factorial expressions, yielding a cleaner form:

Integer-Order Bessel Function Jₙ(x)
Jₙ(x) = (x/2)ⁿ · [ 1/n! − 1/(n+1)!·(x/2)² + 1/[2!·(n+2)!]·(x/2)⁴ − 1/[3!·(n+3)!]·(x/2)⁶ ··· ]

In particular, setting n = 0 gives J₀(x):

J₀(x) — Zeroth Order Bessel Function
J₀(x) = 1 − 1/(1!)²·(x/2)² + 1/(2!)²·(x/2)⁴ − 1/(3!)²·(x/2)⁶ + ···

And setting n = 1 gives J₁(x):

J₁(x) — First Order Bessel Function
J₁(x) = (x/2) · [ 1 − 1/[(1!)(2!)]·(x/2)² + 1/[(2!)(3!)]·(x/2)⁴ − 1/[(3!)(4!)]·(x/2)⁶ + ··· ]
Note 1! = 1. This is worth remembering when evaluating terms like (1!)² = 1 in J₀(x), and (1!)(2!) = 2 in J₁(x), to avoid arithmetic errors.

Topic 2 Active Recall
Application
When v is an integer n, what does the first term inside the bracket of the series expansion for Jₙ(x) simplify to?
It simplifies to 1/n!. For example, in J₀(x) the first term simplifies to 1 (since 1/0! = 1/1 = 1), and in J₁(x) the leading factor (x/2)¹ is outside the bracket while the first term inside is also 1 (since 1/1! = 1).
Comparison
How does the structure of J₀(x) differ from J₁(x) in terms of leading factor and denominator pattern?
J₀(x) has no leading (x/2) factor outside the bracket and uses squared factorials — (1!)², (2!)², (3!)² — in its denominators.

J₁(x) carries a leading (x/2) factor outside the bracket and uses products of consecutive factorials — (1!)(2!), (2!)(3!), (3!)(4!) — in its denominators. This arises directly from setting n = 1 in the general Jₙ(x) formula.
Mechanism
Why does the lecturer specifically note that 1! = 1, and why is this relevant to the Bessel series?
The note clarifies 1! = 1 to help students correctly evaluate denominator terms like (1!)² = 1 in J₀(x) and (1!)(2!) = 2 in J₁(x). Without this reminder, students might incorrectly compute these as larger values, leading to errors in the series coefficients. It is a small but critical arithmetic anchor for the calculations.
