Week 7 of 12 Fueling Supply Systems & Electronic Engine Controls

Fuel must reach the engine's combustion chamber in precisely the right form, amount, and timing. This week covers the two fuel delivery systems (carburetor and fuel injector) and the electronic control systems that manage modern engine performance.

Topic 7.1
The Carburetor

Think of a carburetor like a spray bottle that mixes air and fuel. When you press a spray bottle, liquid gets broken into tiny droplets — that's atomization. The carburetor does the same thing with petrol, breaking it into microscopic droplets and mixing it with air so it can burn properly inside the engine.

The fuel-air mixture delivered by a carburetor to the engine cylinder is indeed in an atomized form.

The carburetor's primary function is to break down liquid fuel into fine droplets (atomize it) and mix it with air, creating a combustible mixture that can be ignited within the cylinder. This atomization is crucial for proper and efficient combustion.
Real-World Example
Older vehicles (pre-1990s in most countries) used carburetors. The carburetor relied on engine vacuum to draw fuel into the airstream — simple and reliable, but imprecise. On cold days, you had to use the choke to richen the mixture because the carburetor couldn't automatically compensate for temperature changes.
Topic 7.2
The Fuel Injector

The fuel injector is like a very precise computer-controlled syringe. Instead of relying on vacuum to pull fuel in like a carburetor, the injector is commanded by a computer (ECU) to spray exactly the right amount of fuel at exactly the right time. It's like the difference between pouring seasoning from a jar (carburetor) versus a chef using a precise dropper (injector).

A fuel injector in an automobile is a critical component that precisely sprays fuel into the engine's combustion chamber to create the correct air-fuel mixture for efficient combustion.

It is essentially an electronically controlled valve that opens and closes to deliver fuel at the right time and in the right amount, determined by the Engine Control Unit (ECU).
Common Mistake
Students say carburetors and injectors do the same thing so it doesn't matter which is used. Major difference: a carburetor uses engine vacuum and is non-adjustable in real time. A fuel injector is electronically controlled and can vary fuel delivery thousands of times per second based on conditions like engine load, temperature, and throttle position — giving dramatically better fuel economy and lower emissions.
Topic 7.3
Electronic Engine Control Systems (ECU)

Modern car engines have a tiny brain called the ECU (Engine Control Unit). It's like a very smart thermostat — it reads dozens of sensors around the engine (temperature, airflow, oxygen levels), and then sends commands to actuators (like the fuel injectors) to keep the engine running at its ideal conditions.

Electronic engine control systems in automobiles utilize Electronic Control Units (ECUs) to manage and optimize engine performance, fuel efficiency, and emissions.

These systems employ sensors to gather data about engine conditions and actuators to adjust engine parameters accordingly, all orchestrated by the ECU.

This technology has revolutionized engine management, replacing mechanical systems with sophisticated electronic control.

The ECU loop: sensors feed data → ECU processes → actuators respond
