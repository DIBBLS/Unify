Week 1 of 11 Magnetic Circuits — Fundamentals

This week introduces the magnetic equivalent of an electric circuit. Every concept here has a direct electrical analogue — once you see the pattern, the whole topic unlocks. The key insight: flux follows magnetic paths the same way current follows wires.

Topic 1.1
What Is a Magnetic Field?

Magnetism shows up in every electrical machine — motors, generators, transformers. You cannot escape it. So let's nail it properly.

Around every magnet (and around every wire carrying current), there exists a magnetic field — an invisible region where magnetic forces act. We visualise this field using magnetic flux lines (symbol: φ, pronounced "phi"). Think of flux lines as the "flow paths" of magnetism.

Four rules about flux lines — know these cold: ① They always form closed loops — they never start or end anywhere. ② They travel from North to South outside the magnet, and South to North inside. ③ They never cross each other. ④ They take the shortest, highest-permeability path available.

The SI unit of magnetic flux is the Weber (Wb).

Think of it like this
Magnetic flux lines are like water flowing in a closed pipe loop — the water always flows in a loop, never disappearing at one end. And just like water prefers wider pipes (less resistance), flux prefers materials with high permeability (less reluctance).
Examinable definition
"Unlike electric field lines, magnetic flux lines always form closed loops — they have no beginning or end."
Topic 1.2
Magnetic Flux Density (B)

Flux (φ) tells you the total amount of magnetism passing through a surface. But flux density (B) tells you how concentrated that flux is — how many Webers pass through each square metre. This is the more useful engineering quantity.

Squash the same flux into a smaller area → flux density goes up. Spread it over a bigger area → flux density goes down. Same total, different concentration.

Magnetic flux density
B = φ / A
B = magnetic flux density (T, where 1 T = 1 Wb/m²)
φ = total magnetic flux (Weber, Wb)
A = cross-sectional area of the magnetic path (m²)
Rearranged: φ = B × A
Think of it like this
Shine a torch at a wall. Total light output (lumens) = flux φ. Move the torch closer — same light, smaller circle on the wall, brighter spot. That brightness per unit area is flux density B. Same φ, smaller A → higher B.
Common mistake
Students confuse φ (total flux, in Wb) with B (flux density, in T). They are not the same thing. B = φ/A. If a question gives flux and area → divide to find B. If it gives B and area → multiply to find φ. Plugging the wrong quantity into the wrong formula is one of the most common ways to lose marks on this topic.
Topic 1.3
MMF, Magnetising Force H & Permeability μ

Magnetomotive Force (MMF) is what drives flux through a magnetic circuit — exactly like EMF drives current through an electric circuit. For a coil of N turns carrying current I: MMF (F) = NI, measured in Ampere-turns (A·t).

Magnetising Force H is the MMF per unit length of the magnetic path. It tells you how much "magnetic pressure" is applied per metre of the path. The same MMF applied to a short path gives a higher H than if applied to a long path.

Permeability (μ) is the magnetic equivalent of conductivity — it measures how easily a material allows flux through it. μ = μ₀ × μᵣ, where μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m is the permeability of free space and μᵣ is the relative permeability of the material (dimensionless). For air: μᵣ = 1. For silicon steel: μᵣ can reach 1000–10000.

MMF, H, and permeability
F = NI    (MMF, Ampere-turns)
H = NI / L = F / L    (A/m)
B = μH = μ₀μᵣH    (links H to B)
N = number of turns · I = current (A) · L = mean path length (m)
μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m (permeability of free space)
μᵣ = relative permeability — dimensionless, material-specific
High μᵣ → easy to magnetise (silicon steel, soft iron)
Worked Example — Finding H

A coil has N=20 turns, I=2A, mean path length L=0.2m. Find the magnetising force H.

Step 1 — MMF

F = NI = 20 × 2 = 40 A·t

Step 2 — Magnetising Force

H = F/L = 40 / 0.2 = 200 A/m

H = 200 A/m
Topic 1.4
Reluctance & Ohm's Law for Magnetic Circuits

Reluctance (ℛ) is the opposition a magnetic path offers to flux — the magnetic equivalent of resistance. A long, thin path through a low-permeability material has high reluctance. A short, wide path through a high-permeability material has low reluctance.

The magnetic Ohm's Law completes the analogy: just like V = IR drives current through resistance, MMF = φ × ℛ drives flux through reluctance. This is the single most important relationship in magnetic circuit analysis.

Reluctance & Magnetic Ohm's Law
ℛ = L / (μ₀μᵣA)    (A·t/Wb)
F = φ × ℛ    (Ohm's Law for magnetics)
L = mean path length (m) · A = cross-sectional area (m²)
μ₀μᵣ = absolute permeability of the material (H/m)
Compare: V ↔ F(MMF) · I ↔ φ(flux) · R ↔ ℛ(reluctance)
The electric–magnetic analogy (frequent exam question)
EMF (V)  ↔  MMF (NI, A·t)
Current (I, A)  ↔  Flux (φ, Wb)
Resistance (R, Ω)  ↔  Reluctance (ℛ, A·t/Wb)
Conductivity (σ)  ↔  Permeability (μ)
Ohm's Law: V = IR  ↔  F = φℛ
Common mistake
The reluctance formula uses absolute permeability μ₀μᵣ — not just μᵣ alone. Writing ℛ = L/(μᵣA) instead of ℛ = L/(μ₀μᵣA) gives an answer that's 4π×10⁷ times too large. Always include μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m in the denominator.
