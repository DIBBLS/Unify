Topic 2.14
India Energy Scenario

India is both a major energy producer as well as consumer. It ranks as world's eleventh greatest energy producer (2.4% of world's total energy production) and sixth greatest energy consumers (33% of world's total energy consumption). Thus India is net energy importer mostly due to large imbalance between oil production and consumption.

As per 2005 data, India's total primary production and consumption of energy was 4 quad watt and 6 quad watt respectively. The per capita primary energy consumption is 600 KGOE (kilogram oil equivalent) for India, where the same in 2450 KGOE for world. Similarly the per capita average annual electricity consumption of India and world is 900 kWh and 2800 kwH.

The total capacity of electricity generation in India is 1.6 × 10⁵ MW produced from the various sources. Industry consumes 49%, Transport 22%, Domestic 10%, Agriculture 5%, and Miscellaneous 14% of power.

Topic 2.14.1
Conventional Sources in India

Fossil Fuel: India has vast reserves of coal (fourth largest in the world). The total recoverable coal is 90 billion tonnes (10% of world's total). The Indian coal has high ash content (25-50%), low heat value (400-500) kW/kg, low sulphur content (1%). India has only 0.6% of the world's oil and gas reserve.

Hydro Resources: India has total potential of 10⁵ MW of which 3.8 × 10⁴ MW have been developed. It is at seventh position in list of nations with hydro resources. Main problems of slow progress in this field are huge installation cost, environmental and social aspect.

Nuclear Resources: The reserve of Uranium is located in Jharkhand state. Out of the total electrical power production nearly 3.0% is being generated by this source. The nuclear power generation is planned to touch 20000 MW by 2020.

Topic 2.15
Power from Nuclear Fusion

Researchers at the Max Planck institute at Greifswald (Germany) planned to inject a tiny amount of hydrogen and heat it till it becomes a super hot gas known as "Plasma" mimicking condition inside the sun.

Fusion is a process in which atoms join at extremely high temperature and release large amount of energy. If above R and D work success it could replace fossil fuels and conventional nuclear fission reactors. At Iter (France) a huge reactor which uses a strong electric current to trap plasma inside a "Doughnut" shaped device (long enough for fusion to take place). The device is known as a "Tokamak" which was conceived by Russian physicists and is considered fairly easy to built. However, its operation is equally difficult.

Lyman spitzer (an America physicist) named the device as Stellarator which has same shape that of Tokamak but uses a magnetic coils instead of a current to achieve the same result.

Although the pursuit of nuclear fusion is an expensive expenditure the R and D work in Germany is in progress which may cost 1.06 billion Euros. It may take nearly 10-15 year to develop and make it a usuable technology.

Topic 2.16
Non-Conventional Resources

India is endowed with abundant renewable energy resources (solar, wind, bio-mass, geothermal and ocean energy sources). Harnessing these resources are best suited to meet energy requirement in rural areas in decentralized manner. India generates 10⁶ MW from non-conventional sources.

Solar Energy: India receives solar energy equivalent to more than 500 trillon kWh/year. The daily global radiation is around 5kWh/m² Day with sunshine ranging from 2400-3600 hours per year.

Bio-mass Energy: The potential for generation of electric power/cogeneration from agro residues is estimated to be nearly 18000 MW and approximately 6000 MW from bagasse through cogeneration. The potential from urban waste is 5000 MW.

Ocean Tidal Energy (OTE): Estimated potential of 12000 MW. Potential sites: Gulf of Kutch (1100 MW), Gulf of Cambay (9000 MW), Sunderban (1300 MW).

Geothermal Energy: Potential of 15000 MW. Nearly 400 potential hot springs have been identified. A 400 kW electric power production plant is under operation at Trattapani.
