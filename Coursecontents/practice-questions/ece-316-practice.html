DC Generator — Theory & Calculations
Q 01 · Theory Excitation in DC Generators
Explain the term excitation in a DC generator. How is it used to classify DC generators?

Excitation is the process of producing magnetic flux in a DC generator by allowing current to flow through the field winding. Without excitation, no flux exists — and without flux, no emf can be generated.

Generators are classified by their excitation method into two broad groups:

  • Separately excited — the field winding receives current from an external DC source independent of the generator itself.
  • Self-excited — the field winding is supplied by the generator's own output. This group is further divided into shunt, series, and compound types.

Permanent magnet generators form a third category where no field winding is used at all — the flux comes from permanent magnets.

Q 03 · Theory Shunt vs Series Field Winding Construction
Why does a shunt field winding have many turns of fine wire while a series field winding has few turns of thick wire? Explain using current and resistance.

Shunt field winding: connected in parallel with the armature, so it operates at terminal voltage. Only a small field current flows through it. Because the current is small, fine wire is sufficient to carry it. However, many turns are needed to produce enough ampere-turns (and therefore enough flux) for the machine to work properly. The resistance is typically high — around 100 Ω.

Series field winding: connected in series with the armature, so it carries the full load (armature) current, which is large. Thick wire is required to handle this without overheating. Only a few turns are needed because the high current already provides sufficient ampere-turns. The resistance is very low — usually less than 1 Ω, to avoid excessive voltage drop.

\(E_g = 230\,\text{V}\)

Q 06 · Theory Compound Generator — What and Why
What is a compound-wound generator? Why is it called compound? State one advantage it has over a pure shunt generator.

A compound-wound generator contains two field windings on each pole — a shunt field winding and a series field winding. The magnetic flux in the machine is produced by the combined (compounded) effect of both windings acting together. That combined action is why it is called compound.

Advantage over a pure shunt generator: a cumulatively compounded generator can compensate for the voltage drop that occurs as load current increases. The series field strengthens flux as load rises, keeping terminal voltage more stable — something a plain shunt generator cannot do as effectively.

Q 07 · Theory Long Shunt vs Short Shunt
Explain the difference between a long shunt and a short shunt compound generator. Which currents change between the two configurations?

Long shunt: the shunt field winding is connected across the armature and the series field winding together — i.e., across the full internal EMF path. The shunt field current is therefore \(I_{sh} = V / R_{sh}\) and the armature current \(I_a\) flows through both \(R_a\) and \(R_{ss}\).

Short shunt: the shunt field winding is connected directly across the armature terminals only — not across the series field. The load current \(I_L\) (not \(I_a\)) flows through the series resistance, giving a slightly different voltage equation.

The key difference is where the shunt winding is tapped. The current distribution — specifically how \(I_{sh}\) is calculated and which current passes through the series field — changes between the two.

Q 08 · Calculation Shunt Generator — Find \(I_{sh}\), \(I_a\), \(E_g\), \(\eta\)

Q 09 · Theory Differentially Compounded Generator
What is a differentially compounded generator? Describe what happens to terminal voltage as load increases.

A compound generator is differentially compounded when the series field winding is connected so that its flux opposes the flux produced by the shunt field winding — rather than aiding it.

As load current increases, the series field strengthens. Because it opposes the shunt field, the total flux in the machine decreases. Reduced flux means reduced generated emf. The terminal voltage therefore falls rapidly and steeply with increasing load — making this configuration unsuitable for most practical applications. It is occasionally used where a drooping voltage characteristic is specifically required.

Q 10 · Theory Why Generator Efficiency Cannot Be 100%
Explain clearly why generator efficiency can never reach 100%. Name all loss categories involved.

Efficiency is defined as \(\eta = P_o / P_i \times 100\%\). For 100% efficiency, output power would have to equal input power — meaning zero losses inside the machine. That is physically impossible because energy is always converted to heat through several mechanisms:

  • Armature copper losses: \(I_a^2 R_a\) — heat generated as current flows through armature resistance.
  • Field copper losses: \(I_f^2 R_f\) — heat in the field winding resistance.
  • Brush contact losses: voltage drop across the carbon brush–commutator interface.
  • Core (magnetic) losses: hysteresis and eddy current losses in the armature iron as flux alternates.
  • Mechanical losses: friction at bearings and brushes, windage from rotating parts.

Because \(P_o = P_i - \text{losses}\), output is always less than input. Therefore \(\eta\) is always strictly less than 100%.

Section B
Armature Reaction & Commutation
Q 11 · Theory Definition of Armature Reaction
Define armature reaction. When does it occur and what causes it?

Armature reaction is the effect of the magnetic field produced by armature current on the main (stator) field flux of a DC machine.

It occurs whenever current flows through the armature conductors — that is, whenever the machine is under load. The armature current creates its own magnetic field. This armature field interacts with the main field produced by the poles, distorting and in some cases weakening it. The result is a combined field that is neither purely the main field nor purely the armature field, but a distorted combination of both.

Q 12 · Theory Two Adverse Effects of Armature Reaction
State and explain the two adverse effects of armature reaction on a DC machine.

1. Weakening of Main Flux (Demagnetisation)

The armature mmf has a component that directly opposes the main field mmf. This reduces the total flux in the machine. In a generator, reduced flux means a lower generated emf. In a motor, it means reduced torque. This weakening effect worsens as load current increases.

2. Distortion of Main Flux (Cross-Magnetisation)

The armature mmf also has a component acting at right angles to the main field — this is the cross-magnetising effect. It distorts the flux distribution across the pole face, making the field stronger under one half of each pole and weaker under the other. The practical consequence is a shift in the Magnetic Neutral Axis (MNA) away from its no-load position, which causes poor commutation and sparking at the brushes.

Q 15 · Theory Compensating Windings
Explain how compensating windings reduce armature reaction. Where are they located and how do they work?

Compensating windings are conductors embedded in slots cut into the pole faces of the main poles. They are connected in series with the armature winding and carry armature current.

Because they carry the same current as the armature but are positioned directly opposite the armature conductors under the pole faces, they produce an mmf that is equal and opposite to the cross-magnetising component of the armature mmf. The two cancel each other out.

The result is that flux distortion under the pole face is eliminated or greatly reduced, the MNA shift is minimised, and commutation improves significantly. Compensating windings are especially important in machines that experience large and rapid variations in load current.

Q 17 · Theory Commutation — Definition and Importance
Define commutation. Why is it important in DC machines? What happens if commutation is poor?

Commutation is the process of reversing the direction of current in an armature coil as it passes through the interpolar region (the region between the main poles), facilitated by the brushes and commutator segments.

Why it matters: DC machines require that the current in each armature coil reverses every half-revolution to maintain a unidirectional output (in generators) or torque in a consistent direction (in motors). Commutation is the mechanism that achieves this reversal.

If commutation is poor: the current reversal is not smooth — a residual emf (reactance voltage) remains in the coil at the moment of switching. This causes arcing and sparking at the brushes. Consequences include: brush wear and damage, commutator pitting and erosion, excessive heat, reduced machine efficiency, and ultimately machine failure if left uncorrected.

Q 18 · Theory Resistance Commutation vs EMF Commutation
Compare resistance commutation and EMF commutation as methods of improving commutation in DC machines.

Resistance commutation uses high-resistance carbon brushes. Because the brush contact resistance is high, the current diverts gradually from one commutator segment to the next as the segment passes under the brush. This smooths the current reversal and reduces sparking. It works reasonably well for lightly loaded machines but does not fully eliminate the reactance voltage problem.

EMF commutation uses interpoles (commutating poles). These small poles are placed between the main poles in the zone where commutation occurs. They generate a small localised emf that exactly opposes and neutralises the reactance voltage in the short-circuited coil. The result is truly sparkless commutation — current reversal is clean and complete. EMF commutation is far superior and is the standard method in modern DC machines.

Section C
DC Motors
Q 21 · Theory Working Principle of a DC Motor
Explain the working principle of a DC motor from the moment supply is applied until rotation occurs. Include the relevant laws.

1. A DC supply voltage is applied across the armature terminals.

2. Current flows through the armature conductors.

3. Each current-carrying armature conductor produces its own magnetic field around it (by Ampere's Law).

4. The main poles (field winding or permanent magnets) produce a separate, steady magnetic field \(F_m\) across the air gap.

5. The interaction between the armature field \(F_c\) and the main field \(F_m\) produces a mechanical force on each armature conductor. The direction of this force is given by the Lorentz force law and practically determined using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule — First finger: field direction; seCond finger: current direction; thuMb: direction of motion.

6. Because the forces on conductors under opposite poles act in the same rotational direction, they combine to produce a net torque on the armature.

7. The armature begins to rotate, converting electrical energy into mechanical energy.

Q 22 · Theory Significance of Back EMF
Explain the significance of back emf in a DC motor. What would happen if back emf did not exist?

Back emf (also called counter emf) is the emf induced in the rotating armature due to its conductors cutting through the main magnetic field — exactly as in a generator. By Lenz's Law, it opposes the supply voltage that caused the rotation.

Its significance:

  • It limits armature current automatically. The effective voltage driving current through the armature is \(V - E_b\), not \(V\). As the motor speeds up, \(E_b\) increases, reducing armature current.
  • It acts as a self-regulating mechanism. If load increases, the motor slows down, \(E_b\) drops, \(I_a\) rises, torque increases to match the load — automatically.
  • It protects the armature winding from overheating due to excessive current.
  • It controls speed: the steady-state speed is largely determined by the back emf magnitude.

Without back emf: at the instant of switching on, the full supply voltage would drive current through only the armature resistance \(R_a\), which is very small. The resulting current \(V/R_a\) would be enormous — enough to burn the windings and damage the machine within seconds. This is why DC motors require a starting resistance at startup, before sufficient back emf has built up.

Q 24 · Theory Derive the Power Equation of a DC Motor
Starting from the voltage equation \(V = E_b + I_a R_a\), derive the power equation of a DC motor and show that mechanical power developed is \(P_m = E_b I_a\).

Start with the voltage equation:

\(V = E_b + I_a R_a\)

Multiply every term by \(I_a\) to convert to a power equation:

\(V I_a = E_b I_a + I_a^2 R_a\)

Identify each term:

  • \(V I_a\) = total electrical power input to the armature
  • \(I_a^2 R_a\) = armature copper loss (heat dissipated in \(R_a\))
  • \(E_b I_a\) = the remaining power — this must be the power converted to mechanical form

Therefore:

\(P_m = E_b I_a = V I_a - I_a^2 R_a\)

This confirms that mechanical power developed equals the product of back emf and armature current. The armature copper loss is the only power lost in the armature circuit itself; everything else goes to the shaft.

Q 25 · Theory Derive the Condition for Maximum Power Output
Derive the condition for maximum mechanical power output of a DC motor and prove that at maximum power, \(E_b = V/2\).

From the armature circuit: \(I_a = \dfrac{V - E_b}{R_a}\)

Mechanical power: \(P_m = E_b I_a = E_b \cdot \dfrac{V - E_b}{R_a} = \dfrac{V E_b - E_b^2}{R_a}\)

To find the maximum, differentiate \(P_m\) with respect to \(E_b\) and set the derivative to zero:

\(\dfrac{dP_m}{dE_b} = \dfrac{V - 2E_b}{R_a} = 0\)

Solving:

\(V - 2E_b = 0 \implies 2E_b = V \implies E_b = \dfrac{V}{2}\)

Therefore maximum mechanical power occurs when back emf equals half the supply voltage. At this point, armature current \(I_a = V / (2R_a)\) — which is very large, making this a theoretical limit rather than a practical operating point.

Q 26 · Calculation Maximum Power Condition
A DC motor: \(V = 240\,\text{V}\), \(R_a = 0.5\,\Omega\). Find:
  • a) Back emf at maximum power
  • b) Armature current at maximum power

(a) At maximum power: \(E_b = \dfrac{V}{2} = \dfrac{240}{2}\)

\(E_b = 120\,\text{V}\)

(b) \(I_a = \dfrac{V}{2R_a} = \dfrac{240}{2 \times 0.5} = \dfrac{240}{1}\)

\(I_a = 240\,\text{A}\)

This extremely high current explains why maximum power is a theoretical condition — in practice a motor is never run at this point.

Q 28 · Theory Fleming's Left-Hand Rule
Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, explain how the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field is determined. Which fingers represent which quantities?

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule applies to motors — it determines the direction of mechanical force (motion) on a conductor carrying current in a magnetic field.

Hold the left hand with the thumb, index finger, and middle finger mutually perpendicular to each other:

  • First (index) finger → direction of the magnetic Field (from N to S pole)
  • seCond (middle) finger → direction of conventional Current flow in the conductor
  • thuMb → direction of the resulting Motion (force on the conductor)

The mnemonic is FBI — Force, B (field), Current — mapped to thumb, first finger, second finger respectively. As long as any two directions are known, the third can be found.

Note: Fleming's Right-Hand Rule applies to generators — it gives the direction of induced emf when a conductor moves in a field.

Q 30 · Theory DC Motor vs DC Generator — Core Distinction
A DC machine can function as both a motor and a generator. Explain the fundamental difference in energy conversion between the two modes and state which law governs each.

DC Generator — Electrical energy from mechanical energy: a prime mover (engine, turbine) rotates the armature conductors through the magnetic field. By Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction, the relative motion between conductors and flux induces an emf in the armature. When a load is connected, current flows and electrical power is delivered. The governing principle is Faraday's Law — a changing flux linkage induces emf.

DC Motor — Mechanical energy from electrical energy: a DC supply drives current through the armature conductors which sit in a magnetic field. By the Lorentz force law (practically applied using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule), a mechanical force acts on each conductor. The combined forces produce a torque, rotating the armature and delivering mechanical power to the shaft.

Both effects coexist in either mode — a generator has back-torque opposing its rotation, and a motor develops back-emf opposing its supply. The machine is always performing both conversions simultaneously; the dominant direction of power flow is what determines whether it is operating as a motor or a generator.
