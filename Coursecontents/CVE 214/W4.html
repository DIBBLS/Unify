Week 4 of 12 Precipitation — Definition, Forms & Types

Precipitation is any water that falls from the sky — not just rain. This week you learn the definition, all 10 forms of precipitation (rain, snow, hail, frost, fog, etc.) and the 4 mechanisms that cause precipitation to occur.

Topic 4.1
Definition & Forms of Precipitation
Exam-ready definition (from textbook)
Precipitation is falling moisture from the atmosphere to the earth as a result of air cooling and saturation.
10 forms of precipitation (from textbook)
1. Drizzle — tiny liquid droplets usually with diameter between 0.1 and 0.5mm with such slow settling rates that they occasionally appear to float.
2. Rain — consists of water drops mostly larger than 0.5mm in diameter. Rainfall usually refers to amount of liquid precipitation. Three intensities: Light (rates of fall up to 2.5mm/hr), Moderate (2.8–7.6mm/hr), Heavy (rate of fall more than 7.6mm/hr).
3. Snow — precipitation in the form of ice crystals. Snow crystals can reach the ground but usually a number of them coalesce (unite) and fall as snow flakes.
4. Hail — precipitation in the form of balls or irregular lumps of ice over 5mm in diameter.
5. Sleet — raindrops frozen while falling. Also when rain falls through air below 32°F, smaller grains of ice known as sleet or ice pellets are formed.
6. Dew — moisture condensed from the atmosphere in small drops upon cool surfaces.
7. Glaze — freezing of drizzle or rain when they come in contact with cold objects.
8. Frost — a feathery deposit of ice formed on the ground or on the surface of exposed objects by dew or water vapour that has frozen.
9. Fog — a thin cloud formed by condensation of atmospheric water (interfering with visibility).
10. Mist — a very thin fog.
Topic 4.2
Types of Precipitation (by mechanism)

Precipitation doesn't just happen randomly — air has to be forced upward and cooled so it can't hold its moisture anymore. There are four main ways this happens:

4 types of precipitation (from textbook)
1. Convectional (Thermal/Convection) Precipitation — occurs in the form of local whirling thunderstorm and is typical of the tropics. The air close to the warm earth gets heated and rises due to its lower density; cools adiabatically to form a cauliflower-shaped cloud which finally bursts into a thunderstorm. Accompanied by destructive winds → called tornadoes.
2. Orographic Precipitation — results from mechanical lifting of air over a mountain barrier; causes heavy precipitation on the windward side.
3. Cyclonic Precipitation — results from lifting of moist air converging into a low pressure belt, i.e. due to pressure differences created by the unequal heating of the earth's surface.
4. Frontal Precipitation — caused when two air masses with contrasting temperatures and densities clash with each other and consequently condensation and precipitation occur. Surface of contact is called a front. Cold air drives out warm air → cold front; warm air replaces retreating cold air → warm front.
Orographic precipitation — air lifted over mountains
Real-world example
The heavy rainfall in western Nigeria (Lagos, Ibadan) compared to the drier north is partly orographic — moisture-laden winds from the Atlantic hit highland areas and dump their rain. The Jos Plateau gets much more rainfall than flat areas at the same latitude for exactly this reason.
Common mistake
Confusing convectional with cyclonic precipitation. Convectional = LOCAL heating causes air to rise (thunderstorm scale). Cyclonic = large-scale LOW PRESSURE convergence forces air upward (weather system scale, hundreds of km wide). Tornadoes = convectional. Monsoon rains = often cyclonic.
