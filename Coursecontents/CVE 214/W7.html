Week 7 of 12 Precipitation Characteristics & Missing Records

Beyond just measuring rain, engineers need to characterise it — how intense is it? How often does it occur? Over what area? And what do you do when a gauge station has missing data? This week answers all of that.

Topic 7.1
Precipitation Characteristics
4.10.1 Intensity (from textbook)
Intensity = amount of precipitation / duration
Rainfall intensity indicates the variation of precipitation with time. The result can be presented in the form of an intensity-duration graph.
4.10.2 Frequency / Return Period (from textbook)
This is the average time period within which a rainfall of specific amount or intensity can be expected to occur once. This is known as the "return period" or the re-occurrence interval.
Re-occurrence interval = the average number of years in which a given event will be equalled or exceeded.
4.10.3 Areal Extent (from textbook)
This concerns the area over which a point's rainfall can be held to apply. Storm totals obviously vary considerably, so the scale of individual catchments determines its importance — for large systems (local thunderstorm, tropical disturbance or extra-tropical depression) and its rate of movement determines the areal extent.
4.10.4 Intensity-Duration Curve
The variation of intensity with time is described with the aid of an intensity-duration curve in which rainfall intensity in mm/hr is plotted against duration of rainfall in minutes.
4.10.5 Intensity-Duration-Frequency (IDF) Curve — General formula
i = KT^x / (t + a)^n [for duration 5–120 min]
For duration over 2 hours: i = c / t^n
i = average intensity (mm/hr) · T = return period (years) · t = duration of precipitation (minutes) · K, x, a, b, n = constants depending on locality

Topic 7.2
Methods of Estimating Missing Rainfall Records

Sometimes a raingauge station breaks down or has gaps in its records. You need to estimate the missing data from surrounding stations. Two methods are used:

Normal Ratio Method (from textbook)
P_x = (1/n) × [N_x/N_A × P_A + N_x/N_B × P_B + N_x/N_C × P_C]
P_x = estimated daily precipitation volume at the missing data station x (depth) · P_A, P_B, P_C = estimated daily precipitation volume at the adjacent stations A, B, C (depth) · N_x = the normal annual precipitation at the missing data station · N_A, N_B, N_C = average annual precipitation at the adjacent stations A, B, C · n = number of surrounding stations used
When to use which method (from textbook)
Use Arithmetic Average procedure when the normal annual precipitations at A, B, C are within 10% of that for station X.
Use Normal Ratio method when the variation is more than 10%.
Check: if N_A/N_x, N_B/N_x, N_C/N_x are all within 1.145 (i.e. within 10%), use arithmetic average.
Worked Example — Missing rainfall data (from textbook)

Station X was inoperative for part of the month during which a storm occurred. Surrounding stations A, B, C were: 978mm, 1120mm, 935mm. Normal annual precipitations: N_A=978, N_B=1120, N_C=935, N_X=1200mm. Find storm precipitation at X.

Step 1 — Check 10% rule:
1120/978 = 1.145 → Not OK (just at limit)
935/978 = 0.965 → OK
1200/978 = 1.226 → Not OK
∴ Arithmetic average procedure will not hold — use Normal Ratio method.
Step 2 — Apply Normal Ratio:
P_x = (1/3) × [(978/978 × 107) + (978/1120 × 89) + (978/935 × 122) + (978/1200 × ...)]
= (1/3)(93.41 + 93.09 + 99.43) = (1/3)(285.93) = 95.32 mm
Common mistake
Always check the 10% rule BEFORE choosing a method. Students jump straight to the Normal Ratio formula without checking. If ALL surrounding stations are within 10% of N_x, the simple arithmetic average is sufficient and faster.
