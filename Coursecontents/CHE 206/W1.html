Topic 1.1
Defining Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the science of energy — specifically how energy behaves as a property of matter and how it gets transformed into heat and work. The key insight is that these transformations aren't random. They're governed by a set of laws that define the absolute limits of what's physically possible. No engineer, no machine, no process can violate them.

There are two ways to study thermodynamics. Classical thermodynamics looks at the big picture — bulk properties like temperature, pressure, and volume that you can measure directly. It doesn't care about atoms or molecules. Statistical thermodynamics zooms all the way in — it explains those bulk properties in terms of what individual molecules are doing. Classical is what engineers use. Statistical is what physicists use to explain why the laws work.

Core definition
Thermodynamics = Science of energy + Laws governing its transformation into heat and work
Classical (macroscopic): net changes, bulk properties, no atomic knowledge needed
Statistical (microscopic): individual particle behaviour, explains why bulk properties exist
Analogy
Think of thermodynamics as the rulebook for energy. Just like a game has rules about what's allowed, thermodynamics defines what energy can and cannot do. Classical thermodynamics watches the game from the stands — it sees scores, patterns, outcomes. Statistical thermodynamics is in the locker room tracking every player's heartbeat. Both are valid; engineers mostly use the stands view.
Common mistake
Saying thermodynamics is "only about heat." It's about energy in all forms — heat, work, internal energy, kinetic energy. Heat is one form. If your exam definition doesn't mention energy transformation and the laws governing it, you'll lose marks.
Exam-ready definition
Thermodynamics is the science concerned with energy as a property of matter, and with the transformation of energy into heat and work. It is principally based on a set of four laws which are universally valid and describe the bounds within which all energy transformations are observed to occur.

Topic 1.2
The Four Laws — What Each One Defines

There are four laws numbered 0, 1, 2, 3 — not 1 to 4. The Zeroth Law was recognised after the others but is more fundamental, so it got bumped to zero. Each law doesn't just state a rule — it defines a core thermodynamic concept. This is the part lecturers love to test.

Four laws — statements and what they define
Zeroth
Defines: Temperature (T)
If system A is in thermal equilibrium with C, and B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
First
Defines: Energy (U) — conservation
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed. For a closed system: ΔU = Q − W
Second
Defines: Entropy (S) — direction of processes
Entropy of an isolated system always increases or stays constant. Heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot.
Third
Gives: Absolute numerical value of Entropy
As a system approaches absolute zero (0 K), entropy approaches a minimum. It is impossible to reach 0 K by any finite number of processes.
Exam-ready — First Law statement (as in CHE 206 slides)
The First Law of Thermodynamics is an expression of the principle of conservation of energy. It states that energy can be transformed from one form to another but cannot be created or destroyed. For a closed system: ΔE = Q − W, where Q is heat added to the system and W is work done by the system.
Exam-ready — Second Law (Kelvin-Planck statement from slides)
It is impossible by a cyclic process to convert the heat absorbed by a system completely into work done by the system. Alternatively: no process is possible which consists solely in the transfer of heat from one temperature level to a higher one.
Common mistake
Mixing up which law defines what. The most common error: saying the First Law defines entropy (wrong — that's the Second Law). Memorise this sequence: Temperature → Energy → Entropy → Absolute value of Entropy (Zeroth, First, Second, Third).
