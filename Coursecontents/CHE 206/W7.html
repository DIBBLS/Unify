Topic 7.1
Heat Engines & The Carnot Cycle

A heat engine is any device that produces work from heat in a cyclic process. The working fluid absorbs heat QH from a hot reservoir, does work W, and rejects heat QC to a cold reservoir — then returns to its original state and repeats. The Second Law guarantees you can never get all of QH out as work.

Heat engine energy balance and efficiency
W = QH − QC   (First Law for a cycle)
η = W/QH = 1 − QC/QH
For a Carnot engine: η = 1 − TC/TH
All temperatures must be in Kelvin. This is the maximum possible efficiency between TH and TC.
Carnot cycle — four steps on P-V diagram
The four Carnot cycle steps
Step 1: a → b — Reversible adiabatic (isentropic) compression

Temperature rises from TC to TH. No heat transfer. Entropy constant.

Step 2: b → c — Reversible isothermal expansion at TH

System absorbs heat QH from hot reservoir at constant temperature TH.

Step 3: c → d — Reversible adiabatic (isentropic) expansion

Temperature drops from TH to TC. No heat transfer. Entropy constant.

Step 4: d → a — Reversible isothermal compression at TC

System rejects heat QC to cold reservoir at constant temperature TC.

Exam-ready — Carnot's Theorem
For two given heat reservoirs, no engine can have a thermal efficiency higher than that of a Carnot engine. The thermal efficiency of a Carnot engine depends only on the temperature levels of the two reservoirs: η = 1 − TC/TH.
Analogy
The Carnot engine is like the theoretical fastest possible car on a given road. It sets the speed limit — no real car can exceed it. Real engines always fall short due to friction, heat loss, and irreversibilities. The temperature difference TH−TC is your "road" — a bigger gap means a higher efficiency ceiling.
Common mistake
Using Celsius instead of Kelvin in the efficiency formula. η = 1 − TC/TH only works in Kelvin. TC = 27°C = 300K. Plugging in 27 and 200 gives a wildly wrong answer. Always convert first.
