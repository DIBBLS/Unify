Topic 2.1
Systems, Boundaries & Types of Systems

A thermodynamic system is simply whatever you decide to study. You draw an imaginary boundary around it, and everything inside is the system. Everything outside is the surroundings. The boundary can be real (like the walls of a tank) or imaginary (like a region of flowing fluid).

Types of systems
Closed systemEnergy (heat + work) can cross the boundary. Mass cannot. Mass is constant.
Open systemBoth energy and mass can cross the boundary. Most real engineering devices.
Isolated systemNothing crosses — no energy, no mass. Purely theoretical.
Adiabatic systemNo heat crosses the boundary. Work can still be done on or by the system.
Common thermodynamic processes
IsothermalConstant temperature (T = constant)
AdiabaticNo heat transfer (Q = 0)
IsobaricConstant pressure (P = constant)
Isochoric/IsometricConstant volume (V = constant, W = 0)
IsentropicReversible adiabatic — constant entropy
IsenthalpicConstant enthalpy
PolytropicPVⁿ = constant (general process)
Exam-ready definitions
A thermodynamic system is a specified quantity of matter and/or a region that can be separated from anything else (surroundings) by a well-defined surface called the boundary.

A thermodynamic process is the succession of thermodynamic states that a system passes through as it goes from an initial state to a final state.

A thermodynamic state is the condition of a system at any instant of time, described by its properties — any quantity whose numerical value depends on the state but not the history of the system.

Topic 2.2
Gibbs Phase Rule — Counting Degrees of Freedom

The Gibbs Phase Rule answers a very practical question: given a system with multiple components and phases in equilibrium, how many variables can you independently change without disrupting the equilibrium? That number is called the degrees of freedom (F).

Gibbs Phase Rule
F = 2 − π + N
F = degrees of freedom
π = number of phases present
N = number of chemical species (components)
Worked examples — exam style
Example 1 — Liquid water in equilibrium with its vapour

N = 1, π = 2 → F = 2 − 2 + 1 = 1. Fix either T or P and the other is determined.

Example 2 — Liquid water + water vapour + hydrogen + oxygen

N = 3, π = 2 → F = 2 − 2 + 3 = 3. Need to fix T, P, and one composition variable.

Example 3 — Water, ethanol, methanol in liquid + vapour equilibrium Past Q CHE 206

N = 3, π = 2 → F = 2 − 2 + 3 = 3

Example 4 — Boiling saturated salt solution with excess crystals Past Q Akinbomi

N = 2, π = 3 → F = 2 − 3 + 2 = 1

Analogy
Think of degrees of freedom like dials on a mixing board. The phase rule tells you how many dials you can turn independently before the system loses equilibrium. F = 1 means one dial — change temperature and pressure is automatically fixed. F = 3 means three dials to play with.
Common mistake
Counting phases wrongly. Solid salt crystals count as a separate phase. Ice, liquid water, and steam = 3 phases (F = 0 — the triple point). Also: N is the number of distinct chemical compounds, not elements.
Exam-ready definition
Gibbs Phase Rule states that F = 2 − π + N, where F is the degrees of freedom, π is the number of phases, and N is the number of chemical species. The degrees of freedom represent the number of independent intensive variables that must be specified to completely define the intensive state of a system at equilibrium.
