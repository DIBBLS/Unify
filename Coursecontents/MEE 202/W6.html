Topic 6.1 Equilibrium Interatomic Distance

In any two bonded atoms there is a distance between the atoms at which the repulsive forces between the atoms are equal to the forces of attraction. This distance is the equilibrium distance — if the distance between the atoms is to be changed then energy has to be spent.

The attractive force is proportional to e²/4πε₀r² while the repulsive force is proportional to e²/4πε₀rᵐ where m is greater than 2. The repulsive force decreases with increasing distances, faster than the attractive force since m > 2.

Force-Distance and Energy-Distance Curves for Bonded Atoms
💡 Think of it this way
Two magnets facing the same way repel (repulsive energy). Two facing opposite ways attract (attractive energy). There's one sweet spot where they sit stably — neither being pushed apart nor snapping together. That's r₀, the equilibrium distance. It's the bottom of the energy well.
Topic 6.2 Packing Polyhedral & Ligancy

A polyhedral is formed if the centers of all the atoms touching a central atom are joined together with straight lines. This describes the arrangement of atoms which visualizes more on the arrangement of atoms in short-range.

For ionic bonding, this is called the ion-polyhedral, which is formed by joining the centers of an-ions surrounding the same cat-ion with straight lines.

Ligancy is the number of the anion/atom that can touch the cat-ion/central atom simultaneously. The smaller the difference of the size — i.e. the higher r/R ratio — the higher the ligancy.

In the hard sphere model, atoms (or ions) are represented by hard spherical balls. The balls are spherical because the distribution of electrons in atoms or ions with a full external shell is spherically symmetrical. The balls are also hard because the strong repulsion between full shells renders each of them impenetrable.

The packing of atoms in materials with non-directional bonds depends on the radius ratio of the atoms involved. The arrangement that lowers the energy of the packed structure must satisfy: (1) each cat-ion must be surrounded by the largest possible number of an-ions; (2) the cat-ions and an-ions must be at their equilibrium inter-ionic spacing; (3) the an-ions do not approach each other more closely than anion's diameter.

Critical Radius Ratio Derivation — Ligancy 3 (Triangular)
⚠ Common Mistake
Students confuse ligancy with coordination number. They are the same thing in this context. Also — when deriving critical radius ratio for ligancy 8 (cubic), students forget the geometry. For a cube, the cat-ion sits at the body centre, touching 8 corner an-ions. The face diagonal = 2Rₐ, the body diagonal = 2(rᶜ + Rₐ). Use Pythagoras: body diagonal = a√3 where face = a√2. This gives rᶜ/Rₐ = 0.732.
★ Why This Matters
Ligancy and critical radius ratio derivations have appeared in every paper from 2011–2023. Know the derivation for ligancy 3 (triangular, r/R = 0.155) and ligancy 8 (cubic, r/R = 0.732). For the exam, also know: why icosahedra are not packing polyhedral — because a regular icosahedron is not a valid space-filling 3D lattice (the angles don't work for periodic tiling).
