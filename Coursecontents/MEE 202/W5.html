Week 5 Ionic, Hydrogen & Van der Waals Bonds

This week completes the bonding picture. Ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals. Hydrogen bond is the strongest secondary bond. Van der Waals is the weakest — but it's responsible for how noble gases liquify and how geckos stick to walls.

Topic 5.1 Ionic Bond

Of the primary bonds, the ionic bond is the only one that does not occur amongst elements to a considerable extent. This is because the ionic bond is essentially an electrostatic force between ions of opposite charge.

The ionic bond is usually formed between elements of low ionization energy (metallic elements) and elements of high electron affinity (non-metallic elements). Like other types of bonds, the ionic bond is formed due to the decrease in the energy of the valence electrons that results from the bonding.

The formation of NaCl from free atoms of Na and Cl can be understood through energy steps:

NaCl Formation — Energy Analysis
Na(gas) → Na⁺(gas) + e⁻    ΔE = +5.14 eV    (Ionization Energy — costs energy)
Cl(gas) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(gas)    ΔE = −3.61 eV    (Electron Affinity — releases energy)
Na⁺(gas) + Cl⁻(gas) → NaCl(crystal)    ΔE = −7.9 eV    (Cohesive Energy — releases energy)

ΔE total = −5.14 + 3.61 + 7.9 = −6.37 eV (net energy decrease = driving force)

Topic 5.2 Secondary Bonds: Hydrogen Bond & Van der Waals

Apart from the primary bonds, there is another group of bonds called the secondary bonds. They are so called because they are weak compared to the primary bonds. Secondary bonds are essentially electrostatic forces between electric dipoles. It is an order stronger when hydrogen is involved.

An electric dipole is formed when the center of the positive charge distribution and the center of the negative charge distribution do not coincide.

Secondary bonds are of two types: the hydrogen bonds and the Van der Waals bond.

Hydrogen bonds are permanent dipolar bonds and they are an order stronger than other dipolar bonds. The hydrogen bond must involve the participation of a hydrogen atom. The hydrogen atom is always the positive end of the dipole — because when a hydrogen atom has an electron removed from it, only a nucleus is left. A nucleus is so much smaller than an atom that the charge distribution density is much higher.

Van der Waals forces arise from atomic or molecular dipoles when there is a Coulombic attraction between the (+) end of an atom and the negative region of an adjacent one. The Van der Waals bond arises from: fluctuating induced dipole bonds (London dispersion), polar molecule-induced dipole bonds, and permanent dipole bonds.

Dipolar bonds that do not involve hydrogen bonds are not permanent — they fluctuate and are non-directional. This force dominates in inert and noble gases, and is responsible for the liquefaction and solidification of some inert and noble gases.

★ Why This Matters
2014/15 asks specifically: "Why is the hydrogen bond in a class of its own?" Answer: because hydrogen, after losing its electron, is just a bare nucleus — extremely small with very high charge density. This makes its bond much stronger (~10×) than other dipolar bonds. Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together, give proteins their shape, and hold DNA strands together.
