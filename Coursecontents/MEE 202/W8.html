Week 8 Miller Indices & Crystal Defects

Miller indices are the coordinate system of crystals — how we name planes and directions. Crystal defects are the imperfections that make materials interesting (and sometimes weak). Both are high-yield exam topics.

Topic 8.1Miller Indices — Planes

The numerical system used to describe the points, planes and directions in a crystal are called crystallographic indices. The indices l, m, and n are called Miller indices.

6-Step procedure to find Miller indices of a plane:

  1. Set up a right-handed x-y-z coordinate system. If the plane passes through the origin, take a new origin at an adjacent corner.
  2. Find where the plane intersects each axis (in units of a, b, c). If parallel to an axis, intercept = ∞.
  3. Take the reciprocals of these intercepts.
  4. Multiply by the lowest common factor to get the smallest integers.
  5. Enclose in parentheses (hkl) — no commas. Negative values get a bar over the number.
  6. For a family of equivalent planes, use curly braces {hkl}.
Key Crystallographic Planes in a Cubic Cell
Families of Planes & Directions
Family of planes {hkl} — all crystallographically equivalent planes.
{100} includes: (100), (010), (001), (1̄00), (01̄0), (001̄) — the six faces of a cube.

Family of directions <uvw> — all equivalent directions.
<110> includes: [110],[101],[011],[1̄10],[1̄01],[01̄1],[11̄0],[101̄],[011̄] — 12 directions

Planar density = number of atoms centred on plane / area of plane in unit cell
Linear density = number of atoms centred on direction / length of direction
⚠ Common Mistake
Planes use ( ) parentheses. Directions use [ ] square brackets. Families of planes use { } curly braces. Families of directions use < > angle brackets. Mixing these up costs marks. Also — a negative intercept is written as a bar OVER the number, not as a minus sign before it. You cannot write (-1,0,0); you write (1̄00).
Topic 8.2Crystal Defects

A crystalline defect is the lattice irregularity having one or more of its dimensions on the order of an atomic diameter. The imperfection or irregularity of the geometrical arrangement of atoms in a crystalline solid is called a crystalline defect.

TypeDimensionsDefinitionExample
Point Defect0D (a point)Interruption in the regularity of a crystal lattice at a pointVacancy, Interstitial, Impurity
Line Defect1D (a line)Interruption in the regularity of a crystal lattice along a lineEdge dislocation, Screw dislocation
Surface Defect2D (a surface)Interruption in the regularity over an areaGrain boundary, Twin boundary
Volume Defect3D (a volume)Interruption in the regularity in some volumeVoids, Cracks, Inclusions

Vacancy: The simplest form of point defect. It refers to an empty, unoccupied site in a crystal lattice. It can arise from imperfect arrangement of atoms during crystallisation and also from thermal vibration of atoms at high temperature. Hence vacancy is thermally dependent and increases with temperature.

Number of Vacancies — Equilibrium
Nv = N · e−Qv/kT
N = total number of atomic sites (atoms/m³) · Qv = energy required for vacancy formation (eV) · k = Boltzmann's constant = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/(atom·K) or 8.62 × 10⁻⁵ eV/atom·K · T = temperature in Kelvin

Example: Find equilibrium vacancies for copper at 1000°C
T = 1273 K, Qv = 0.9 eV/atom, atomic weight = 63.5 g/mol, density = 8.4 g/cm³
N = (density × NA) / atomic weight = (8.4 × 6.02×10²³) / 63.5 = 7.96 × 10²² atoms/cm³
Nv = 7.96×10²² × e^(−0.9/8.62×10⁻⁵×1273) = 2.2 × 10¹⁸ vacancies/cm³
Point Defects — Vacancy, Interstitial, Schottky & Frenkel

Schottky vs Frenkel: The Schottky imperfection is associated with the vacancy of both an-ion and cat-ion. The Schottky imperfection is more likely to occur than Frenkel defects because it is favoured by electrical neutrality — and only few ionic solid cat-ions have the space to dissolve cat-ions without considerable strain.

Dislocations (Line Defects): A linear disturbance in one dimension of the geometry. There are two types: Edge dislocation (an extra half-plane of atoms inserted in the crystal) and Screw dislocation (lattice displaced in shear). Properties of edge dislocation: (1) has a Burgers vector perpendicular to the dislocation line; (2) moves parallel to the applied stress; (3) can climb by absorption/emission of vacancies; (4) two edge dislocations of opposite sign on same slip plane will attract and annihilate each other.

Dislocation Interaction Rule
Two dislocations can interact to form a third if the reaction LOWERS energy.
Criterion: |b₃|² < |b₁|² + |b₂|² (energy of resultant < energy of reactants)

Example: Can [123] and [2 0 1] interact?
b₃ = [123] + [201] = [3 2 4]
|b₁|² = 1²+2²+3² = 14 · |b₂|² = 2²+0²+1² = 5 · |b₁|²+|b₂|² = 19
|b₃|² = 3²+2²+4² = 9+4+16 = 29
Since 29 > 19, interaction is NOT possible (would increase energy).

Example: Can [1̄35] and [134] interact?
b₃ = [-1+1, 3+3, 5+4] = [0 6 9] — check: 0+36+81=117 > (1+9+25)+(1+9+16)=61 → NOT possible.
★ Why This Matters
Dislocation interaction questions have appeared in 2011, 2015, 2019 and 2021 papers. Always: (1) add the Burgers vectors to get b₃; (2) calculate |b|² for each; (3) compare sum of reactants to product. If product energy > sum of reactants → NOT possible. State what reaction IS possible if asked.
